Featured case study

SiDLY Healthcare · ongoing

SiDLY Health: one record, three people

SiDLY Health connects the people around a single visit: the care manager who plans it, the carer who makes it and the resident who receives it. I lead its UX/UI across the mobile apps and the web platform.

The challenge

One visit, three people, three apps that have to agree on the same data.

What I did

Led UX/UI for the mobile apps and the web platform, prototyped in React/TypeScript.

Signature move

Visits start and end with an NFC scan, so hours are settled from real time.

Lead UX/UI designerWeb + mobileReact/TS prototypeMedical device · MDR IIa

A visit belongs to all three

When the carer finishes a visit, it appears in the resident's history, ready to be rated, and in the manager's record of work. An alarm handled by the manager disappears from every view.

The screens

Care managers run their team from the phone as much as from the desk. This flow covers the carers: who they look after, when they work and what they can access.

Carers use the Planner on their phone during the visit. The flow is built around the scan: a visit starts when the phone touches the resident's tag and ends the same way.

The resident sees the same visits from the other side: who came, what was done, and a place to say how it went.

At the desk the same data gets more room. The manager plans and settles the team's work, carers can follow their visits from a laptop, and residents see their own record.

The product ships in Polish, so the screens are in Polish. All names and data are test data.

Key design decisions

  1. A visit starts with a scan

    The carer starts and ends each visit by scanning the resident's NFC tag with their phone. Work time is recorded as it happened, not as it was planned, and carers' hours are settled from it.

  2. The care card has a lifetime

    Before a visit the care card is the plan. During the visit the carer fills it in. After the visit it becomes the record that the resident and the office read, so it is locked as it was when the visit ended.

  3. The resident's home screen answers three questions

    Am I all right, what do I need to do today, and when is someone coming? Large text, large touch targets, few words, and SOS always on top.

  4. Urgent first, then the day

    The manager's dashboard opens with what needs a reaction, then today's visits in the field, the week ahead and equipment. Each block leads to the screen where that issue gets solved.

  5. Recurring visits stay ordinary visits

    A series is a set of normal visits that share a tag, so any single one can be moved or cancelled without breaking the rest.

  6. Prototype in code, not in frames

    The flows are built as a clickable React/TypeScript prototype with realistic test data, so managers and carers test whole visits instead of single screens.

Case study

SiDLY Healthcare · in progress

Redesigning the ERP that runs public telecare

Municipalities, public institutions and care providers use the SiDLY ERP to order telecare bands, assign them to residents, keep consent documents in order and renew contracts. I am leading its redesign.

The challenge

Routine tasks where mistakes are easy to make and hard to notice, for very different clients.

What I did

Research with municipal and foreign clients, then a clickable React/TypeScript prototype.

Signature move

An undo instead of a confirm for anything reversible.

Lead designer1,000+ client organisationsReact/TS prototypeIn progress
The problem and how I workedHide

The problem

Most of the work in the ERP is routine: activating a batch of bands, swapping a broken one, attaching a signed consent form. Mistakes in these tasks are easy to make and hard to notice. A band can be activated for the wrong client, or keep sending data under a person who no longer wears it.

The system also serves two very different clients. A Polish municipality works in złoty, uploads documents from its social-welfare office and tracks coverage of its residents aged 60+. An export client sells across borders, invoices in euro and needs dialling codes per country.

How I worked

Weekly project meetings with stakeholders, plus research with municipal and foreign clients about how they order, assign and renew devices.

I built the redesign as a clickable prototype in React and TypeScript with realistic test data, so whole flows can be tested instead of static mockups.

Where the decisions sit

The life of an order and its bands, simplified. Numbers match the decisions below.

Key design decisions

  1. Confirm only what is expensive

    Asking twice for everything trains people to click through. A confirmation now appears only for a new paid year or a change to many devices at once. A single suspend or resume is reversible, so it gets an undo instead.

  2. Read the file back before acting on it

    Bulk activation comes in as a spreadsheet, with columns in a different order every time. Rows are matched on the device number, and nothing runs until each line has been shown back with what it will do. The template you download is the file you can upload.

  3. Keep a rule where it matters

    Health data needs signed consent, but the clerk entering a resident rarely has the scan at hand. Blocking the form only taught people to upload placeholder files. Now the resident can be saved, is flagged "no consent" everywhere, and cannot receive a band until the scan arrives.

  4. Every number opens its list

    Each count on the dashboard opens exactly the devices it counts, and each bar in the renewals chart renews what it shows. Nothing on the dashboard sends you hunting elsewhere.

  5. Show what an action will touch

    With a mixed selection, each action states how many of the selected devices it would change and switches off at zero. The selection bar used to be the one dark element on the screen, which made ticking three rows feel like a warning. It is now calm, with one obvious next step.

  6. Never rely on colour alone

    Every status carries an icon and a label as well as a colour, because green and amber are hard to tell apart for colour-blind users.

The interface is unreleased and confidential, so this case study describes the process and decisions without screens. I am happy to walk through the prototype in an interview.

HackYeah 2026 · Sport & Healthcare challenge

Lemi: health care without the stress

A health guide with a friendly lemur. It helps every day and at every doctor's visit: it joins mood, movement, medication, measurements and test results, and turns them into one small step for today.

The problem

People put off the doctor because of the phone call, the ten minutes and the advice they forget.

What I built

A web app and a Flutter app on the Claude API and ElevenLabs, designed and coded at the hackathon.

Signature move

One recorded visit becomes three summaries: for you, for family, for the doctor.

Why I built it, and the numbers behind itHide

It started with me. I'm a student and I put off calling the doctor, not because I don't know what is wrong, but because I have to call, explain myself, remember everything in ten minutes and then keep track of the advice. At the hackathon I asked other people whether they felt the same. It was far more common than I thought, and each conversation added a group the app could help: people for whom Polish is not a first language, people with disabilities, neurodivergent people, seniors and their families, people with health anxiety.

  • Measurements from a photo: point the camera at a blood pressure monitor, glucometer or scale, AI reads the display and you confirm the value.
  • Record the visit with the doctor's consent and get a summary in three versions: for you, for someone close to you, and for the doctor.
  • Test results from a PDF or photo, with every parameter explained in plain language.
  • "Call for me" with AI, a simple mode with large text for people 50+, and a calm mode.

Featured project

NLP · B.A. thesis · 2026

Unmasked: catching manipulative UX copy with NLP

"Only 2 left!", "43 people are viewing this", "No thanks, I don't like saving money." Dark patterns live in the words. I trained a classifier to spot them and shipped it as a Chrome extension that warns you before you buy.

The problem

Manipulative copy is everywhere in e-commerce, and people rarely notice it.

What I did

Trained and compared five classifiers on 2,356 labelled texts, then shipped the best as a Chrome extension.

Result

F1 0.936 and ROC AUC 0.976, running fully offline in the browser.

Method: data, features and why rules in the extensionHide
2,356 texts · 50/50 balanced · Mathur et al., 2019TF-IDF · 1–2-grams · 5,000 featuresSWPS University · Psychology & CS

What the model learned

Dark patterns use their own vocabulary: urgency ("hurry", "limited"), scarcity ("only", "left", "stock") and social proof ("people", "purchased"). Classical models on TF-IDF did surprisingly well; the rare categories suffered from too little data.

Why rules in the extension

The top features of the trained model were encoded as explicit rules, so the extension classifies fully offline, with no API calls. It highlights flagged copy on the page by severity and explains the psychological mechanism behind each one.

Data engineering

Streaming · CDC · lakehouse

UX friction detection, in real time

A data platform that watches how people use an app and flags friction as it happens: rage clicks, abandoned sessions, screens people get stuck on. Every interaction is captured from the database with change data capture and processed as a stream.

Data model

users, screens (with a UX goal, expected time and whether they sit on the critical path), sessions (converted, abandoned, total friction score) and ux_interactions, where each event can be marked as friction.

Mini data platform

Analytics engineering

SQL · DuckDB

Where do users drop off?

A reproducible SQL ELT for product funnel analytics. Raw tracker events go in; clean, explained UX metrics come out, ready for a dashboard.

Bronze keeps the raw JSON as it arrived. Silver applies explicit data-quality rules. Gold joins sessions to funnel steps and produces conversion per step and CTR and conversion per device, so a product team can answer which step loses the most people, which device converts best and how much of the traffic is bots.
